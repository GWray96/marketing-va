A marketing audit is the fastest way to understand the real state of your business's marketing. You can do a useful one in an afternoon. Here is exactly how.

## What is a marketing audit, and why should you do one before anything else?

A marketing audit is a structured review of everything your business is currently doing to attract, engage, and convert customers — including the gaps where nothing is happening at all. For most small businesses, doing one before spending any more money on marketing is the single most valuable thing they can do.

The alternative — picking a new tactic and hoping it works — is what most founders default to. It is also why most marketing spend at small businesses produces disappointing results. Without knowing what you have, what is broken, and what your customers actually see when they find you, every new initiative is built on guesswork.

A good audit does not take weeks. It takes honest attention for a few hours.

## Step 1: Audit your website as a stranger would see it

Start by opening your homepage in an incognito browser window — no logged-in bias, no cached customisations. Ask yourself these questions as if you are a potential customer who has never heard of you.

Is it immediately clear what you do and who you do it for? Not in a general sense — specifically. If someone landed here after searching for the service you offer, would they immediately understand you are relevant to them?

What is the first thing the page asks you to do? Is there a single clear call to action, or are there five options competing for attention? Multiple options for the visitor usually means no choice gets made.

Check your contact page, your about page, and at least one service or product page. Do they all tell a consistent story? Is it easy to get in touch? Does the page load quickly on a phone? (Use Google's PageSpeed Insights — it is free and will show you a score and the main issues.)

Write down what is clear, what is confusing, and what is missing. Be honest. What you discover in this step usually explains more about underperforming enquiries than any amount of advertising spend.

## Step 2: Check whether your tracking is actually working

Most small businesses either have no tracking set up, or have tracking that was set up once and has never been verified. Both are a problem.

Go into Google Analytics 4 (or whatever analytics platform you use) and check whether traffic is being recorded. Look at the last 90 days. Is the data plausible, or are there gaps — periods of zero sessions, sudden drops, wildly inconsistent numbers? These are signs of a broken installation.

Check whether goal completions are being tracked. If someone fills in your contact form, does that register as a conversion? If not, you have no way of knowing which traffic sources are actually generating leads versus which ones just look busy in a dashboard.

If you run paid ads, verify that your conversion tracking is working in both the ads platform and your analytics. Paid ads without conversion tracking is money spent with no way to measure return.

Note down what is tracking correctly, what is broken, and what is not set up at all. Do not try to fix everything today — just know what you have.

## Step 3: Map your active marketing channels

Make a list of every channel your business is currently using. Email. Organic social. Paid ads. SEO. Referrals. Partnerships. Events. PR. For each one, answer three questions.

Is it actually active? Posting twice a month does not count as active social media marketing. An email newsletter that has not gone out since last autumn is not a channel you are using.

Is it generating anything measurable? Traffic, enquiries, bookings, revenue. If you genuinely do not know, that is the finding — this channel is running without accountability.

Is the output consistent with your brand and your current positioning? Old content often reflects who you were two years ago, not who you are now.

What you will almost certainly find — based on what I heard from thousands of business owners during my year qualifying SMBs for eBay's Start Up – Scale Up programme — is that most channels are technically live but practically dormant. They exist because someone started them. Nobody owns them.

## Step 4: Review your follow-up process

This step gets skipped in most audits because it does not feel like marketing. It is actually where most small businesses lose the most business.

When a lead makes contact — fills in a form, sends an email, calls the office — what happens next? How quickly do they hear back? What does that first response look like? Is there a second touchpoint if they do not reply?

A surprising number of small businesses generate inbound enquiries and then lose them through slow or inconsistent follow-up. This is not a marketing problem; it is a systems problem. But fixing it can produce more revenue than any new campaign, because you are recovering value from leads you already paid to attract.

Map out the current follow-up journey for your most common enquiry type. Write down where it is working well and where it goes silent.

## Step 5: Look at what your competitors are doing

This step should take 30–45 minutes, not days. You are looking for patterns, not a comprehensive report.

Pick three competitors — ideally businesses your customers would realistically consider alongside you. Look at their website, their social profiles, and their Google search presence. Are they running ads? Do they have a blog? How often do they post? What do their reviews say?

You are looking for two things: what they are doing consistently that you are not, and what they are doing badly that you could do noticeably better. Both are useful inputs for your own marketing plan.

Do not use this step to generate anxiety. Use it to generate specific, actionable observations.

## What to do with your findings

By the end of the afternoon, you should have a clear picture of where your marketing function actually is. Not where you planned for it to be — where it is.

Most founders who do this exercise come away with the same realisation: the gaps are not primarily about tactics. They are about ownership. The website has not been updated because nobody owns the website. The email list has not been touched because nobody owns email. The social posts stopped because nobody owns social.

The answer to that is not another tool or another tactic. It is somebody owning the whole function.
