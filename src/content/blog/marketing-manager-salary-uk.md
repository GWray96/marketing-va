Hiring a marketing manager in the UK costs significantly more than the salary figure on the job advert. For a small business weighing up its options, understanding the true number is the only way to make a sensible decision.

## What does a marketing manager salary in the UK actually look like?

Most UK marketing manager roles sit somewhere in the mid-£30Ks to mid-£40Ks range, based on published job board data and annual salary surveys from UK marketing bodies. Junior roles and regional positions tend to come in below £35,000. Experienced managers in larger cities, or those with specialist skills in paid media or marketing automation, can sit well above £45,000.

These figures shift year on year and vary substantially by sector, so treat them as a directional range rather than a precise benchmark. What matters for a small business is not the average — it is what you actually need to budget, in total, from day one.

## The number employers underestimate: total cost of employment

The salary is the starting point, not the ending point. When a small business hires a marketing manager at £38,000, here is what the full picture looks like.

**Employer's National Insurance** is currently charged at 13.8% on earnings above the secondary threshold. On a £38,000 salary, that is roughly £4,200–£4,500 a year in employer NI contributions alone. The precise figure changes with thresholds and rates — check the current HMRC guidance before you budget.

**Pension contributions** under auto-enrolment require a minimum employer contribution of 3% of qualifying earnings. On a £38,000 salary, that is around £900–£1,000 a year on top.

**Recruitment costs** for a quality marketing manager hire typically run to one or more months of salary if you use a specialist recruiter, or several weeks of founder time and job board spend if you do it yourself. Neither is free.

**Software and tools.** A marketing manager doing their job properly will need access to a range of platforms. Email marketing software, SEO tools, social scheduling platforms, project management tools, design tools, analytics — when you stack them up, even a modest toolkit commonly adds hundreds of pounds a month to the total.

**Hardware and equipment.** A laptop, monitor, and the desk it sits on are not dramatic costs, but they are real ones.

Add these together and the commonly cited rule of thumb — that the true cost of employment runs to 1.3–1.5x headline salary — holds for most small businesses. On a £38,000 salary that puts your total annual commitment somewhere in the £50,000–£58,000 range before you have bought a single tool or paid a recruiter.

## The ramp time problem

There is a cost that does not appear in any spreadsheet: the time between a marketing manager starting and the point at which they are genuinely productive.

A new hire needs to understand the business, the customers, the competitive landscape, the existing assets, and the brand voice. They need to audit what is already in place, build relationships with suppliers, and develop a plan they believe in. In a small business without a dedicated marketing team around them, this process is slower, not faster — because there is nobody to onboard them except the founder.

A realistic estimate for a new marketing hire to be operating at close to full capacity is three to six months. During that period, you are paying a full salary for partial output. That is not a criticism of marketing managers — it is just how embedding into a new business works.

The implication is that the total first-year cost is higher still. You are carrying recruitment cost, full salary from day one, and reduced productivity for the first quarter at minimum.

## When the in-house hire is still the right answer

None of this means you should never hire a marketing manager. If your business generates enough consistent marketing work to fill a full-time role — and you have the budget, the management time, and the patience for a proper recruitment and ramp process — an in-house hire is excellent. Over time, that person builds institutional knowledge of your business and your customers that no external partner can fully replicate.

The question to ask is whether you genuinely have full-time work right now, or whether you have occasional bursts of activity that could be covered more efficiently by a different model. Many 1–25-person businesses are in the latter camp. They need marketing momentum — consistent output across email, content, SEO, and social — but not necessarily a permanent full-time seat to generate it.
