For a small business trying to get marketing moving, there is no universally right answer between agency, freelancer, and in-house — but there are clear wrong answers depending on your situation. This is an honest comparison of all three, including where each genuinely wins.

## What is the real question here?

The right question is not "which option is best?" It is "what does my business actually need right now, and which model delivers that?"

Most 1–25-person businesses need two things simultaneously: strategic direction and consistent execution. They need someone who can look at the business, decide what marketing should be doing, and then get on and do it. When you frame it that way, the options look quite different.

The cost ranges for agencies are commonly published across UK industry resources; individual agency pricing varies considerably and you should always ask for a breakdown of what a retainer actually covers.

## When an agency genuinely wins

Agencies are the right call when you need specialist depth at scale. You are spending £30,000 a month on Google Ads and you need a team of certified specialists managing bidding, creative testing, and conversion tracking with rigour. You are a 100-person business that needs a PR agency with press relationships you cannot build yourself. You need a technical SEO overhaul on a 10,000-page ecommerce site.

At those levels, the agency model earns its fees. The problem is that most agencies do not have a genuinely "small business" tier — they have a small-business price and a small-business account executive who is managing twelve other clients simultaneously. The quality you pay for at the top of the rate card rarely trickles down to a £1,500 a month retainer.

The other watch-out: most agencies own one channel. They will do your SEO brilliantly. Meanwhile your email list is silent, your social has not been touched in six weeks, and there is no coherent message tying any of it together.

## When a freelancer genuinely wins

A skilled freelancer is often the best value in the market for a well-defined, contained job. You need 12 blog posts written to a specific brief. You need a Google Ads account set up and handed over. You need a new website designed. Hire the right freelancer, give them a clear scope, and you will get excellent output at a fraction of agency prices.

The problem starts when you try to use several freelancers to cover your whole marketing function. Now you are the project manager. You are coordinating the copywriter, the SEO person, the social media manager, and the email specialist. You are setting the strategy. You are quality-checking every brief. You have just given yourself a second job — which is precisely what you were trying to avoid.

I spoke to thousands of business owners during my year qualifying SMBs for eBay's Start Up – Scale Up programme. The "scattered freelancers" setup was one of the most common things I heard described as something that "didn't really work." Not because the freelancers were bad — they often were not. Because the coordination and strategic layer was being absorbed by a founder who already had a business to run.

## When in-house genuinely wins

An in-house marketing hire is the right answer when you have enough work to fill that person's time every single day, a budget that comfortably covers salary plus employer's National Insurance, pension, and software (more on the real total in [the marketing manager salary piece](/blog/marketing-manager-salary-uk)), and the patience to wait for a good candidate and give them time to get up to speed.

If those three conditions are true, a full-time marketing manager is excellent. They embed in the business, they know the customers deeply, they build institutional knowledge over time. The model works.

It usually stops working at the small end because the business does not have a full-time marketing workload yet, or the budget is not there for a quality hire, or the founder does not have time to manage and develop a junior. A £28k marketing coordinator without proper oversight will produce activity without results.

## The gap in the middle

What strikes me when I look at all three options is how often none of them quite fits a 6–20-person business. Too small for a proper agency retainer to make sense. Not enough defined projects to use freelancers cleanly. Not enough scale for a full-time hire to be justified.

That gap — strategy plus full-function execution in one person, at a monthly subscription cost that sits below a salary — is exactly what the [Marketing Executive VA model](/pricing) is built to fill. One experienced operator who owns the whole function, coordinates nothing because they are doing it themselves, and works on a rolling monthly contract with no long-term lock-in.

It is not the right answer for every business. If you are already spending serious money on paid ads and you need specialist depth, keep your paid-ads agency. But if you are at the point where marketing just needs someone to own it and get it moving, the comparison above tells you what the options really look like.
